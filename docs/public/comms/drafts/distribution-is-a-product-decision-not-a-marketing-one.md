# Distribution Is a Product Decision, Not a Marketing One

*September 2026*

We made a choice early on about how people would actually use Piper Morgan: no dedicated app to download, no separate login to create, no chat window we'd have to design and maintain ourselves. Instead, it shows up inside the chat tools people already have open — Claude, ChatGPT, whatever they're already living in day to day.

The easy way to describe that choice is as a marketing decision. Fewer barriers, lower friction, more people willing to try it. All true, and all beside the point. It was a decision about what the product actually is, not about how many people would see it.

# What changes when the surface changes

A standalone app and a plugin that lives inside someone else's chat interface aren't the same product wearing different clothes. They're different products, with different constraints baked into their bones.

A standalone app can initiate. It can ping you, remind you, show up in your notifications before you asked it to. A plugin that lives inside a chat interface generally can't — it only gets to speak when spoken to, structurally, by the design of the surface it lives on. That's not a limitation someone forgot to build around. It's a real property of choosing where you live, and it changes what kinds of promises the product can honestly make.

Choosing distribution means choosing which shelf's physics your product has to obey — not packaging a finished thing for a new shelf.

# The test that's currently proving the point

We're in the middle of listing Piper Morgan on a plugin marketplace right now, and the test case turned out to be more honest than any hypothetical would have been.

The listing describes a hosted connection — the same "meet people where they are" idea, extended to a backend service instead of a downloaded app. Before writing a word of that description, the actual question was whether the thing being described exists yet, not which sentence would read best.

It doesn't. Not in any form a stranger could actually reach today. The acceptance criteria for the hosted endpoint sit at zero of fifteen checked. The code has the pieces for calling other services, and the shared plumbing underneath, but no server anyone outside our own team could actually connect to. There is no listing that could honestly describe a product that isn't running.

That's not a copywriting problem to solve with careful wording. It's the same principle from the other direction: no amount of marketing polish turns something into a real product before it's actually shipped. A product exists when it's in production, not when the description of it reads well.

# Why this matters beyond one listing

The instinct to treat distribution as a marketing layer — something you bolt on after the real work is done, to get the real work in front of more people — misses that the shelf changes the product before a single customer ever sees it. Committing to a surface that can only respond, not initiate, is a constraint on what the product is allowed to promise, decided the moment you pick where it lives — not a go-to-market detail.

That's the actual argument for treating distribution as product work from the start, not handing it off once the "real" decisions are made. By the time you're writing the listing copy, the decisions that matter have already happened. All that's left is whether you're honest about what they actually built.
