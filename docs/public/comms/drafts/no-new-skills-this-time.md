# No New Skills This Time

*October 23 – November 6, 2026*

As I've tried to build an always-improving system, one thing I added was a regular sweep to see if we need to make any new skills. This most recent time we didn't find anything new to "skillify" and what we did find was better handled as an addition to an existing process.

## Self-improving systems

Back in July I was reflecting on the way the Piper Morgan development process catches its own errors, devises process fixes, and generally exhibits what I called "emergent self-improvement and self-healing behaviors. Some from the model upgrades perhaps but others from our own harness." 

I was curious where some of that facility was coming from and we agreed to a monthly skills review to capture things the system is teaching itself and to ratify or "pave the cowpath" where appropriate.

I don't want to accumulate governance for its own sake, so the review was designed sorts ideas into three categories:

- build
- fold into something existing
- don't build

The don't-build option derives from what the agents call my  "don't-overlearn principle" which has to do with not overengineering a solution to an rare or low-impact problem that can more easily be handled by existing review and repair processes.

# Physician, heal thyself!

Then we forgot to run the review in September or October. (This is ironically that same sort of issue about what it takes for a good idea to actually happen or to be done consistently. Whether or not it requires a skill, it clearly needs a trigger or hook.)

My chief innovation agent (CIO) ensured any future skipped cycle would be visible: the GitHub issue generated by a workflow action as a reminder now gets a comment when a cycle comes due and is skipped. 

# The manual review

My chief of staff agent (Exec) gathered skill candidates reviewing "Wanted but not found" lines in session logs, the omnibus logs, and its own view across the team from Sep 1 to Oct 23. This added ten new candidates to three from July that had never been reviewed.

Exec offered to discuss with me and I was tired on October 23. They logged the plan to discuss it "tomorrow, when he's fresh" but then I didn't really get to it till November 6 when I reviewed the full set of recommendations and approved them all. One item had already addressed on November 4. Of the rest, we decided not to turn any others into skills, to fold five of them into exiting processes, and to drop the other six.


**6. The finding.** "Nothing became a skill. Most signal pointed at a missing line in an existing place, or at a tool or permission, not a procedure." And two of Exec's leans were already stale by review day, because the system had moved on (seats now wake from a scheduler, not session timers, and a push script already existed). CIO caught both by checking against the actual files before you saw them. [l.74, l.63, l.66]

**7. It's back on schedule.** Next cycle due Tue Dec 1. [l.74]

## Careful with these (accuracy)
- **13 candidates, but "12 leans".** 13 were reviewed (10 new plus 3 from July). You accepted 12 leans on Nov 6. The 13th (the skill-scope audit) was already ruled and done on Nov 4 on your "Yes finish it". Say "thirteen ideas" for the count, or "a dozen" loosely. Don't say "twelve candidates".
- "Folded" items did get built, as small edits (a line in a briefing, a step in a runbook). The claim is no *new skill*, not no change.
- "Every week" and "8 days running" are counts of log files that mention a topic, which "overstate chatty topics" (l.49).
- The skill count: 34 skills with a SKILL.md (counted 11-06). The "36" in the doc's 10-23 gather was wrong.

## Your angle, maybe
- Agents (like people) ask for new process when a sentence in the right place would do.
- The value of a review is in the don't-build column. A review that adds nothing keeps the system small.
- Wanting a skill vs. wanting permission: several "skills" were really requests for access or a seat.
