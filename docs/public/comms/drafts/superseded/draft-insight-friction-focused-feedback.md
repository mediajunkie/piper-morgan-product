# Friction-Focused Feedback

*March 26, 2026*

*March 19*

Nine roles. Nine questionnaires. Nine responses. One hundred percent response rate.

On the first deployment.

That's not normal. Organizational feedback mechanisms typically launch to low participation, generic answers, and wish lists. Ours produced specific, evidence-grounded friction reports that led to three methodology documents being created the same evening.

The difference was in the design.

## Ask what's hard, not what's good

The Agent 360 questionnaire — our quarterly feedback mechanism — had explicit ground rules:

"Focus on friction, not satisfaction. 'What's hard?' is more useful than 'what's good?'"

"Only suggest improvements that don't require more PM time or attention than currently exists. The PM is already at capacity."

"Be specific. Cite documents, issues, or omnibus entries where possible."

"'I don't have enough context to answer this' is a valid response."

And at the bottom, a plausibility check before submission: Is this based on specific observed friction, or theoretical concern? Could this be implemented by agents without PM involvement? Does this require more PM time?

The constraints weren't limitations. They were *focus*.

## What we heard

All nine roles independently cited briefing staleness. Five of nine said their predecessor's handoff memo was more useful than the official briefing document for orientation. Four cited PM-as-mailbot latency — waiting for the PM to relay messages between roles.

These weren't surprises. We'd already started fixing the briefing staleness two days earlier. The mailbot bottleneck was being addressed by the Mailbox v3 system under construction that same day.

But the 360 responses did something the fixes couldn't: they *validated the priority*. We weren't fixing imaginary problems. Nine independent observers confirmed the same friction points.

The responses also surfaced things we hadn't noticed. The Communications role pointed out that the Weekend publication cadence — a core operational rhythm — was undocumented. It had emerged through practice and been transmitted through handoff memos, but it didn't exist in any process document. If a handoff memo missed it, the rhythm would break.

## Why the plausibility check matters

The most important design element was the plausibility check. Every suggestion had to pass through a filter: does this require more PM time?

This single constraint eliminated the most common failure mode of organizational feedback — the wish list. When people are asked "what would make your job better?" they naturally generate ideas that require someone else to do more work. Better documentation. More context. Faster responses. Richer briefings.

Those suggestions are valid but useless. The PM is the bottleneck. Adding work to the bottleneck doesn't reduce friction — it increases it.

The plausibility check forced a reframe. Instead of "what would help me?" the question became "what could change without adding to the PM's load?" The answers were structurally different: self-serve access to the editorial calendar instead of asking the PM to upload it. Documenting the Weekend publication cadence instead of relying on oral tradition. Cross-referencing publication state against the CSV instead of asking the PM at session start.

Each suggestion reduced PM burden rather than increasing it. That's not what happens when you ask "what do you want?" It's what happens when you ask "what's hard, given the constraints we actually operate under?"

## From friction to methodology in one evening

HOSR synthesized the nine responses and identified seven cross-cutting themes. From those themes, three action items were created — each maximally specific, each assigned to a role that could execute without PM involvement.

"Formalize the Colleague Test with a definition, rubric, and examples." Not "improve testing." A specific artifact with specific components.

"Document the Roundtable Synthesis process with a step-by-step workflow, facilitator checklist, and template with historical case studies." Not "document our processes." A specific methodology gap with a specific format.

Both were completed the same evening. The CXO delivered the Colleague Test formalization with a three-dimension scoring rubric and five worked examples. The PPM delivered the Roundtable Synthesis methodology with three case studies from project history.

The cycle — questionnaire → synthesis → action items → delivered methodology — completed in a single day. Not because anyone rushed. Because the friction-focused design produced actionable findings that didn't require centralized decision-making to act on.

## The design principles

If you're building a feedback mechanism — for AI agents, for human teams, for any organization — the friction-focused approach has four design principles:

Ask about friction, not satisfaction. "What's hard?" produces actionable answers. "What's good?" produces praise.

Constrain suggestions to the system's actual capacity. If your bottleneck is the manager, don't collect suggestions that require more management. If your bottleneck is engineering time, don't collect suggestions that require more engineering.

Require evidence. "I feel like the briefings are stale" is a sentiment. "BRIEFING-ESSENTIAL-COMMS.md references GREAT-3B, which ended four months ago" is a finding.

Include a plausibility check. Make the respondent evaluate their own suggestions against the constraints before submitting. This shifts the cognitive work from the synthesizer to the respondent, where the domain knowledge actually lives.
