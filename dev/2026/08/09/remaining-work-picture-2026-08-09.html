You moved beta because more work remained than anyone reported. Here is why the reports couldn't have shown it — and what the real number is.

The finding

The beta-gate count fell from 26 to 17 in two days. In the same two days, 48 new issues were filed with no milestone at all.

Every one of those 48 was created on or after August 7. They are invisible to the beta-gate count by construction, because the count reads the MVP milestone and these carry no milestone. So the number everyone reports went down while the work went up, and no one was lying.

"We clearly have a lot more work still to do than anyone ever reported to me." — the reports were structurally incapable of showing it.

This is the denominator problem in its most consequential form yet. Last week's version excluded six never-started items inside the sprint. This version excludes an entire category of work that sits outside every milestone — and it is the fastest-growing category we have.

What the 48 actually are — and this part is good news

They are not scope creep. They are the product of you testing the product. Three on Aug 7, twenty-five on Aug 8, twenty on Aug 9 — the discovery rate rose as you spent real time in it.

  • The alpha-feedback conversion — five issues, filed this morning, zero unfiled. The strongest one is cold-start: show the user their own work in the first exchange, which two independent lanes converged on.
  • Your own testing session — todo CRUD that is incomplete and lies about it, reminder duration-words silently dropped, a single malformed row taking down an entire journal, all-day events rendering as "at 12am."

My read: this is a healthy week that looked like a bad one only because the instrument pointed at the wrong bucket. Finding forty-eight real defects by using the product two days before shipping it to strangers is the system working. Shipping without finding them would have been the failure.

The sort — what needs it, what dimensions are real, three approaches

Six items need sorting, and the reason there's no answer yet is that the old key was withdrawn rather than replaced.

The key everyone was sorting against — "which surface survives the plugin pivot" — was withdrawn yesterday after you corrected its premise: the web UI is not going away, and the experience is "not specific to any one surface… expressed on each surface as appropriate." Nothing replaced it. So the five alpha-feedback issues and one bug sit unmilestoned, and the ordering logic that would justify a tier is gone.

Everything else was triaged this morning. This is the residue that needs a principle, not a decision-by-decision call.

The dimensions that are actually real

Four hold up under examination. I've marked which discriminate well and which don't, because a dimension that splits nothing is decoration.

  • Does fixing it change the value answer? — i.e. does it make "this is just an LLM with extra UI" unavailable as a reading. Discriminates sharply: #1536, #1538, #1539 do; #1537, #1540, #1511 do not, however real they are.
  • Does the fix travel across surfaces, or land on one? — the salvageable version of the withdrawn key. You rejected "which surface survives," but "is this experience-level or surface-local" follows directly from your own framing. #1536 and #1539 travel (every surface has a first contact and a purpose). #1538 travels as a pattern. #1537, #1540, #1511 are web-local.
  • Did it change the tester's behavior? — the welfare carve-out, and the honest discriminator between real and aesthetic. #1537 did (he wrote a message he couldn't read). #1540 did (he didn't find navigation). #1539 did (anxiety, in his words). #1536 arguably did the most — he never met the differentiator.
  • Evidence strength — #1536 alone has independent convergence from two lanes that didn't coordinate. The rest each rest on one lens. Weak as a primary sort (it would rank a single sharp observation below a popular one) but a good tiebreak.

One dimension I'd retire: "preference vs. structural." Two of these six were filed as preference and corrected to structural on review. A category that gets overturned a third of the time is measuring the filer's confidence, not the work.

One we don't have: cost. None of the six is sized. Any sort that leans on effort is currently guessing, and I'd rather say so than imply a cheap/expensive split exists.

Three approaches, with what each gets wrong

  • A · Value-first. Sort by whether the fix changes the value answer, dependency-ordered within tiers. Gives: #1536 → #1539 → (#1537 → #1538) → #1540 → #1511. Gets wrong: pushes two behavior-changing bugs below a conceptual fix, and #1538 is arguably the deepest change while sitting third because its blocker is cheap.
  • B · Behavior-change-first. Ship what demonstrably altered how the tester acted, then the value work. Gives: (#1537 → #1538) → #1540 → #1536 → #1539 → #1511. Gets wrong: optimizes for the one tester we have. Four of these travel to every future user; two are what one practitioner hit on one afternoon.
  • C · Travel-first. Ship what's experience-level before what's surface-local, since the surface-local work will be re-litigated as other surfaces mature. Gives: #1536 → #1539 → #1538 → #1537 → #1540 → #1511. Gets wrong: leaves #1537 late even though it blocks #1538, which is a mechanical contradiction the sort has to override.

My recommendation: C with the dependency honored — #1536, #1539, then #1537→#1538 as a pair, then #1540, with #1511 sorted separately as a plain bug rather than experience work.

Why C: it's the only one derived from a principle you've already stated rather than from the tester's session, so it keeps working when Jake is one of twenty testers instead of one of one. Why the caveat: #1537 costs little and unblocks the deepest item, so treating it as "surface-local, therefore later" would be the sort overriding a fact. And #1511 is not an experience finding — two features answering to one name is a plain defect, and sorting it alongside FTUX work would flatter it.
