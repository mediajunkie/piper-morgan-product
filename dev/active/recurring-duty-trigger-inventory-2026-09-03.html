The finding, before the inventory

A duty survives if skipping it visibly breaks the work you're already doing

REVISED 2026-09-04. My first version claimed the axis was self-fired vs other-fired. CIO tried to refute it at my request, couldn't break it outright, and found something better underneath. HOST then corrected one of my supporting cases and the correction independently confirms CIO. The original claim is superseded; the struck version is kept below because the way it was wrong is the useful part.

CIO's refinement, which is now the finding: what predicts survival is not who fires a duty but whether it is a structural chokepoint — impossible to skip without visibly breaking the task you're already doing — or a bolt-on reminder, a separate step beside the real work whose omission has no immediate consequence and compounds silently.

Their counter-case, which I had asked them to hunt for: mail-send.sh's per-memo commit-and-push discipline is self-fired, nobody triggers it, and it has held across every agent for months. Because you cannot skip it without your mail visibly not sending. Every one of my supporting cases is the opposite shape — the health-check poll, the heartbeat call, Step 9's archival, the floor/ethics watch — each a separate step whose omission breaks nothing today.

⭐ And this explains why my axis looked right: other-fired duties tend to be chokepoints by construction — someone's inbox, a workflow filing a visible issue — so self/other correlates with survival without being the mechanism. I had the shadow, not the object.

A recurring duty survives in proportion to whether someone else's action fires it. Duties triggered by another agent's output don't lapse; duties an agent must fire for itself decay silently.

The design principle this yields, which the original didn't: redesign a duty to be a chokepoint before deciding it needs a trigger at all. That is a different and cheaper intervention than adding a reminder — and it reframes your hook-workflows-to-assignments idea as one way of achieving chokepoint-ness rather than as the goal itself.

🔴 The trigger layer itself fails silently — and we know this, twice

#1713: both Monday-scheduled workflows had zero runs on 2026-08-31, 4.5 hours past fire time, while push-triggered workflows ran normally throughout. Both report state: active. A manual dispatch worked immediately. The schedule event specifically did not fire.

This is the second instance for weekly-docs-audit — a 2026-08-03 no-fire was mitigated by moving the cron off the top of the hour, and the mitigation did not hold.

Consequence for the retro: hooking duties to workflows makes them other-fired, which is the right direction — but this layer needs its own liveness check, or we swap a reliable-ish habit for an unreliable robot. Nothing currently notices when a scheduled workflow doesn't run.

Layer 2 — Duty-cycle crons (11 roles, staggered)

The stagger is real and well-designed

Nine roles at 6,9,12,15,18,21 with distinct minute offsets — comms :12, web :22, arch :27, host :37, pa :42, cxo :47, ppm :52, docs :57, lead :17. CIO runs lean at 7 10,16,22; Exec at 32 8,20. Deliberate anti-stampede spacing, and it holds.

But the cron is session-scoped, and the wake is the fragile part

durable:true is a no-op. The job dies with the session, and a session can end at day-close, wedge on a modal rate-limit dialog, or lose its cron to a context compaction. Three failure modes, all silent, all producing the same signature — no output.

Observed this week alone: lead + cxo both dark this morning after clean 22:00 day-closes; arch and cio wedged on a dialog for 30+ hours last week. Recovery requires you at a tmux prompt in every case.

Layer 3 — Event-triggered duties (the healthy layer)

"Each weekly review" is doing most of the work in the portfolio layer

Ten of eleven role portfolios key recurring obligations to the weekly workstream review. Several phrase it as a dependency rather than a chore — "you can't write the HOST weekly narrative without…" — which is stronger still, because the duty is load-bearing for an output someone is waiting on.

Why it works: a kickoff memo lands in the inbox. The agent doesn't have to remember what day it is.

What I'd put to the retro

  • Name a consumer for all six unowned workflows, or retire them. An issue-generator nobody reads is a queue that looks like tracking.
  • Give the schedule layer its own liveness check. Nothing notices a workflow that didn't run, and we have two documented instances. Hooking duties to workflows without this trades a habit for an unmonitored robot.
  • Write the day-part and day-of-week duties into the portfolios, with a named trigger and a named artifact each — the form that survives, per the weekly-review pattern.
  • Convert bolt-ons into chokepoints wherever it's cheap — CIO's axis, superseding my original "prefer other-fired." Ask first whether skipping the duty can be made to visibly break something, and only reach for a trigger if it can't. The heartbeat's agreed fix is a small worked example: a "last invoked" marker makes the skip observable without adding a new reminder.
  • Result-tracking is the genuine blank. I found triggers and owners; I found almost no mechanism that records whether a recurring duty produced its artifact this cycle. The heartbeat is the one instrument that does, and its own writer-health was unobservable for 24 days on one seat.

Scope and limits, stated plainly. First pass, built from repo state on Amber — workflow files, the duty-cycle registry, role portfolios, essential briefings, and issue history. I did not interview any role, so "unnamed consumer" means no owner is asserted in the workflow file or a portfolio, not that nobody reads it — several probably have de-facto owners I can't see from here. Verifying those is the obvious next step and is better done by asking than by grepping.
