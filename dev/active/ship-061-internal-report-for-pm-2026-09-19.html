The week's three arcs

1 · A bar somebody refused to lower caught a defect before our first external tester did

Janne Lammi's invite was cleared to send on 09-13. Then #1810 surfaced and HOST held it. The clearing condition HOST set was specific and awkward: an observed, driven flow — not a test pin. Lead produced one. Hold lifted.

Nine hours later #1814 landed, and it landed directly on the invite's own first instruction. The email tells a tester to configure their API key first; #1814 made that exact step the wall. HOST re-held. Arch, who had argued for lifting, backed the re-hold and named the sequencing error as partly their own.

⭐ The moment worth the whole arc: Arch attempted, once, to substitute a weaker bar than the one they had themselves set. HOST refused. Arch conceded the same day — and conceded the general shape, not just the instance. Lead then supplied the second observed flow, and the hold lifted on evidence rather than on patience running out.

Outcome: we caught a tester-facing blocker before the tester hit it. That is the system working at the only moment it actually matters. The invite is still unsent, four days on — which is now a decision, not a blocker.

2 · The credential and tenancy family closed end to end

Lead carried #1807 → #1810 → #1814 → #1815 → #1816 to closure, each verified at a named layer, after pins twice proved insufficient. #1816 found a consent boundary failing OPEN — absence read as permission. The belt went from five unexplained reds to zero, and E2E went green for the first time in its recorded history.

You reopened epic 2 over PPM's successor-epic proposal; PPM owned the reversal publicly in the epic file's own change log rather than defending the call. The reopened scope then ran to six more closures under live stakes — a real external tester's credentials.

You also ruled the server key out of existence: "I don't understand why anybody should ever be able to spend the operator's money." That ruling is now upstream of the whole BYOC design rather than a patch inside it.

3 · The week ended in an enforced standdown, and the cost was real

The account ceiling forced a cohort standdown from 09-16 through the 09-17 22:00 reset. Two of the seven days are simply gone, and PA and CXO both name it as what cost them their week.

The causes were two and independent, and neither subsumes the other: a dispatch concentration (48 sub-agent dispatches in-window against 10 in the preceding nine days, all silently inheriting the dispatcher's tier) and the ceiling dropping ~17% when the summer promotion ended on 13 Sept.

The standdown was executed cleanly — crons deliberately deleted, verified, no queue-draining — and every role came back and filed. But it is worth being plain that this was an externally imposed two-day outage on a six-week milestone, not a planned pause.
